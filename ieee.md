# A Literature Survey on Hybrid Blockchain-IoT Architectures for Transparent and Accountable Public Distribution Systems

Jueli Chintawar, Vishwesh Patil, Vansh Barde, Himanshu Mire: Students, Computer Engineering Department, St. Vincent Pallotti College of Engineering & Technology (SVPCET), Nagpur, India.
Dr. Kapil Gupta: Associate Professor, Computer Engineering Department, St. Vincent Pallotti College of Engineering & Technology (SVPCET), Nagpur, India.

**Abstract—** India's Public Distribution System (PDS) delivers subsidized food grain to more than 800 million beneficiaries through nearly 500,000 Fair Price Shops, yet two decades of digitization efforts—including Aadhaar seeding, electronic point-of-sale devices, and the One Nation One Ration Card initiative—have not eliminated leakage, diversion, authentication failure, stock manipulation, or the absence of real-time oversight. This persistence points to a structural weakness rather than a lack of effort: centralized relational databases record transactions but remain mutable by design, so digitized records have not become tamper-evident or independently verifiable. This survey critically reviews and compares forty-eight sources across fifteen technology areas relevant to trustworthy welfare distribution, including blockchain-based ledgers, food-supply-chain traceability, Internet of Things-based physical verification, digital governance frameworks, biometric and token-based authentication, cryptographic hash chaining, smart contracts, hybrid blockchain-cloud architectures, artificial-intelligence-based fraud and anomaly detection, digital wallet systems, role-based access control, and cloud-hosted government computing. Rather than identifying an absence of suitable technology, the analysis reveals an integration gap: each mechanism required for a trustworthy distribution system already exists in validated form somewhere in the literature, yet no surveyed system combines more than two or three of these mechanisms into a single deployable architecture, even among the closest hybrid blockchain-cloud precedents. This finding motivates the proposed research direction—a Digital Supervision Layer that unifies hybrid blockchain anchoring, physical quantity verification through Internet of Things sensors, layered multi-factor authentication, ledger-aware role-based access control, an auditable digital wallet, and streaming rather than retrospective anomaly detection into one coherent transaction pipeline, addressing the accountability weaknesses that isolated point-solutions have consistently failed to resolve.

**Keywords—** blockchain, Internet of Things, public distribution system, hash-linked ledger, anomaly detection, role-based access control, multi-factor authentication, digital wallet

## I. INTRODUCTION

### A. Background of India's Public Distribution System

India's Public Distribution System (PDS) is among the largest food-security programmes in the world, distributing subsidised rice, wheat, sugar, and kerosene to more than 800 million beneficiaries through a network of roughly half a million Fair Price Shops (FPS) operating under state civil supply departments and the Food Corporation of India (FCI) [1]. Historically, the system relied on paper ration cards, manual stock registers, and physical verification at the shop level — a design that made the supply chain opaque at precisely the points where diversion is easiest: warehouse dispatch, transport, and shop-level dispensation [14]. The enactment of the National Food Security Act (NFSA), 2013, converted PDS entitlements into a legal right for nearly two-thirds of the population, which sharply increased the administrative and audit burden on a system that was never built for that scale of accountability [1], [3].

### B. Need for Digital Transformation

The digitisation of PDS — beginning with the End-to-End Computerisation of TPDS Operations scheme and continuing through Aadhaar seeding, ePoS devices at FPS counters, and the One Nation One Ration Card (ONORC) portability initiative — was driven by the recognition that manual record-keeping could not be reconciled against real allocations in near real time [16], [13]. While ePoS rollout improved point-of-sale authentication, it did not, by itself, create a verifiable and tamper-evident record of the entire supply chain from FCI godown to beneficiary household; ePoS logs remain siloed within state-run servers that are themselves editable by the departments that operate them [14]. This gap between "digitised" and "trustworthy" is the central motivation for research into cryptographically verifiable and IoT-instrumented PDS architectures, which forms the core subject of this survey.

### C. Current Challenges

Despite two decades of computerisation efforts, several structural weaknesses persist across the PDS pipeline:

- **Leakage** — the diversion of allocated foodgrain between the warehouse and the ration shop before it reaches beneficiaries, estimated in multiple state-level studies to still affect a meaningful share of allocated stock despite ePoS adoption [3].
- **Diversion** — grain recorded as dispensed to genuine cardholders but actually siphoned to the open market, a fraud mode that survives digitisation when the digital record itself can be falsified at the point of entry [5].
- **Authentication failures** — biometric mismatch rates among elderly and manual-labour beneficiaries continue to cause wrongful denial of entitlements under Aadhaar-based verification [18].
- **Stock manipulation** — mismatches between physical stock and recorded stock at FPS level, enabled by the fact that current systems record transactions but do not independently verify dispensed *quantity* [12].
- **Poor transparency** — beneficiaries and civil-society auditors have no independent means of verifying that the ledger they are shown has not been altered after the fact [21].
- **Lack of real-time monitoring** — state dashboards largely operate on periodic batch uploads rather than continuous transaction-level visibility, delaying fraud detection until well after diversion has occurred [15].

### D. Motivation

Each of the challenges above traces back to a common architectural weakness: PDS digitisation to date has focused on *recording* transactions rather than making those records *tamper-evident* and *independently verifiable*. A centralised relational database, however well administered, is fundamentally mutable by design — any actor with sufficient database privileges can retroactively alter dispensation records without leaving a structurally enforced trace. This is precisely the property that blockchain-based ledgers, cryptographic hash chaining, and IoT-based physical verification are designed to eliminate. The motivation for the present research is therefore not to replace the existing PERN/PostgreSQL-based operational stack, but to layer a hybrid, hash-linked, blockchain-anchored audit trail atop it — one that preserves the performance and cost characteristics of a conventional relational backend while adding cryptographic non-repudiation at the points in the pipeline where fraud has historically concentrated.

### E. Scope of Survey

This survey examines fifteen technology areas relevant to the design of a trustworthy digital supervision layer for PDS: blockchain applications in public distribution and food supply chains; IoT-based smart ration hardware; digital governance frameworks for welfare delivery; Aadhaar and QR-based authentication; cryptographic hash chains; Ethereum smart contracts; hybrid blockchain architectures; AI/ML-based fraud and anomaly detection; digital wallet systems; supply chain transparency mechanisms; role-based access control; and cloud-based government computing. Priority is given to work published between 2020 and 2025, supplemented by foundational papers where they remain the primary reference point for a technique. The survey deliberately excludes papers concerned purely with agricultural procurement or crop-yield forecasting, as these lie outside the distribution-and-audit scope of the proposed Digital Supervision Layer.

## II. LITERATURE SURVEY

This section presents a structured and critical review of fifteen research directions that collectively inform the design of a digital supervision layer for the Public Distribution System, spanning blockchain-based trust mechanisms, Internet-of-Things (IoT) enabled physical verification, authentication and access-control schemes, and artificial-intelligence-driven anomaly detection.

### A. Blockchain in Public Distribution Systems

Efforts to graft blockchain and IoT technologies onto India's ration-distribution machinery have converged on a shared diagnosis - that centralized record-keeping enables short weighing, stock diversion, and unverifiable audit trails - while diverging sharply in scale, technical depth, and empirical rigor. Malathi et al. [4] offer the most operationally mature response, deploying a private Ethereum network across Raspberry Pi nodes with Proof-of-Authority consensus rather than Proof-of-Work, and coupling it to QR-code-based ration-card authentication at IoT-enabled checkpoints so that grain movement can be tracked from warehouse to shop counter. Distinctively, the system layers a Tamil voice/text interface atop a NodeJS/REST/Web3 backend, explicitly targeting usability for illiterate and semi-literate rural beneficiaries rather than treating accessibility as an afterthought. A ten-day field trial with 80 participants in Anjur village yielded encouraging figures - full transaction immutability, roughly 130 ms response latency, 100-250 ms block-generation delay, and about 90% user satisfaction - demonstrating that blockchain-IoT integration is technically feasible even on constrained hardware [4]. Yet this feasibility was established only at village scale: throughput degrades once load exceeds roughly 40 requests per second because of fixed block size, and per-transaction delay grows with each additional node, leaving nationwide or multi-state performance unverified [4].

By contrast, Shwetha and Prabodh [5] and Singh et al. [6] operate at the conceptual rather than implementation level, and in doing so expose complementary gaps. Shwetha and Prabodh [5] propose an intelligent tracking scheme in which IoT sensors log commodity arrival and dispatch at warehouses and fair-price shops directly onto a blockchain ledger, positioning immutability and decentralization as a substitute for the existing centralized tracking apparatus; however, as a short conference contribution, it reports no throughput, latency, or deployment-cost figures, and does not address consensus design or beneficiary-side authentication [5]. Singh et al. [6] instead tackle the systemic fragmentation among government agencies, warehouses, shop owners, and beneficiaries by proposing a consortium (permissioned) blockchain with smart contracts and an explicit on-chain/off-chain split - recording trust-critical transactions on-chain while offloading bulk data off-chain to balance transparency against scalability [6]. This on-chain/off-chain philosophy anticipates the architectural pattern later required at national scale, but Singh et al. [6], like Shwetha and Prabodh [5], stop short of any prototype, field pilot, or quantitative evaluation, and neither incorporates real-time IoT-based physical verification of dispensed quantities or AI-driven anomaly detection.

Read together, these three works trace an arc from small-scale empirical validation [4] to conceptual, unvalidated architectural proposals [5], [6], collectively establishing that blockchain-IoT audit trails are desirable but leaving open how such trails scale nationally, integrate with AI-based fraud detection, or interoperate with existing digitization initiatives. The proposed Digital Supervision Layer borrows the on-chain/off-chain separation of trust-critical data from bulk records [6] and the IoT-based physical-checkpoint philosophy of [4] and [5], but implements it as a hybrid architecture anchoring only transaction hashes on Ethereum Sepolia while keeping beneficiary data in PostgreSQL, replaces ad hoc QR-checkpoint tracking with weight-verified dispensing via load-cell/HX711 hardware, and adds an AI/analytics anomaly-detection module that none of [4], [5], or [6] provide - directly addressing the scalability, AI-augmentation, and empirical-validation gaps each identifies.

### B. Blockchain for Food Supply Chains

Early efforts to bring tamper-evident traceability to agri-food supply chains converged on pairing blockchain's immutable ledger with automatic data-capture technologies, and Tian's conceptual architecture for China's agri-food sector exemplifies this first wave [7]. By weighing the respective strengths and weaknesses of RFID against blockchain and then fusing them into a single layered design, the proposal enables identification, inquiry, tracking, monitoring, and tracing of goods as they move through production, processing, warehousing, distribution, and selling [7]. Its contribution lies less in empirical validation than in framing the problem: it establishes that whole-chain, multi-stakeholder trust requires both a reliable sensing mechanism at each node and a shared, tamper-resistant record of what was sensed [7]. However, being a 2016 design-stage proposal, it offers no working implementation, no throughput or latency evaluation, and no treatment of RFID cost, tag failure, or blockchain scalability, leaving the architecture unvalidated at production scale and silent on last-mile welfare distribution to individual beneficiaries [7].

Pincheira Caro et al. move decisively from concept to practice with AgriBlockIoT, a decentralized traceability system that integrates IoT data producers and consumers directly into smart contracts, deployed and benchmarked on both Ethereum and Hyperledger Sawtooth for a farm-to-fork use case [8]. Where Tian's work stops at architecture, this study quantifies real trade-offs — latency, CPU usage, and network usage — across a public versus a permissioned blockchain, providing rare comparative evidence relevant to platform selection [7], [8]. This empirical rigor comes at the cost of scope: the evaluation remains confined to a small emulated testbed rather than a multi-stakeholder national deployment, and the generic commercial use case incorporates neither AI-based anomaly detection nor beneficiary/entitlement identity verification, leaving open how such decentralized IoT-blockchain integration would generalize to subsidized public distribution [8].

Prashar et al. narrow the geographic and policy focus to India directly, proposing a smart-contract-driven traceability and visibility system for agricultural products that stores verified transactions on IPFS rather than relying on a central intermediary [9]. Their experimental evaluation, reporting 161 transactions per second with 4.82-second convergence, gives the strongest quantitative performance evidence among the three works and is the most contextually proximate to an Indian deployment [9]. Yet, like [7] and [8], it addresses general market traceability rather than ration-card entitlement verification, omits IoT-based physical quantity sensing, and leaves governance and permissioning models unspecified [9].

Collectively, [7]-[9] establish that blockchain-based traceability is both conceptually sound and practically implementable, but none extends into last-mile welfare distribution, couples blockchain with physical dispensation verification, or incorporates AI-driven anomaly detection. The proposed Digital Supervision Layer borrows the hash-anchored, tamper-evident ledger principle common to all three [7]-[9], adopts the hybrid off-chain/on-chain separation and empirical performance sensibility demonstrated in [8] and [9], and directly closes the identified gap by anchoring PDS transaction hashes on Ethereum Sepolia while adding IoT-based weight verification and AI-based anomaly detection absent from all three precedents.

### C. IoT-based Smart Ration Distribution

A substantial body of embedded-systems research has sought to digitize the fair-price-shop counter itself, moving verification and dispensing away from paper ledgers and manual scooping toward sensor- and identity-driven automation. Shukla et al. [10] represent an early instance of this trend, proposing an RFID-based smart ration card in which each beneficiary's paper card is replaced by a unique RFID tag that a microcontroller-driven reader at the shop authenticates against a backend database before releasing the family's monthly quota and updating consumption records in real time. The three-tier design — tag, reader/microcontroller, and cloud database — succeeds in eliminating duplicate and ghost cards and produces a remotely auditable digital log, but the work remains a lab-scale hardware prototype: it neither engages with rural connectivity failures nor with the risk of RFID tag cloning, and, critically, it offers no mechanism for verifying that the dispensed quantity actually matches the logged entitlement, nor any tamper-evident ledger beyond a conventional database [10]. Gaikwad and Nikumbh [11] pursue a parallel but technologically distinct path, pairing a smart card encoded with household entitlement data with a GSM module that authenticates the cardholder at the shop counter and pushes SMS confirmations to the beneficiary's registered number. While this GSM-based notification loop meaningfully increases beneficiary awareness and reduces disputes over quantities received, the system inherits the fragility of cellular coverage in remote areas and, like [10], stores no biometric binding, leaving the card vulnerable to loss, duplication, or intermediary misuse, and it offers no cross-verifiable record between government, dealer, and citizen [11]. Indirani et al. [12] advance this line further by discarding the physical card altogether in favor of Aadhaar-linked fingerprint authentication combined with an STM32-controlled automated dispensing mechanism and OTP verification via GSM, logging transactions to a cloud-hosted Google Spreadsheet. This biometric binding directly addresses the impersonation and card-duplication weaknesses of [10] and [11], and sensor-controlled dispensing improves weighing precision over manual scooping; yet the choice of a spreadsheet as the system of record is itself a regression from an integrity standpoint, offering no access control granularity or tamper-evidence suitable for state-scale deployment, and the paper, like its predecessors, stops short of any distributed-ledger or AI-based anomaly detection layer [12].

Read together, these three works trace a clear trajectory — from RFID identity replacement [10], to GSM-mediated notification [11], to biometric authentication with automated dispensing [12] — each incrementally hardening beneficiary identification and shop-counter automation, yet all sharing the same structural blind spot: none verifies dispensed weight against entitlement in a way that is independently auditable, and none anchors transaction records in a tamper-evident, multi-stakeholder ledger. The proposed Digital Supervision Layer borrows the core insight of embedded, sensor-driven verification at the point of dispensing from [10]-[12], but replaces card- or biometric-only authentication with an ESP32/load-cell/HX711 weighing subsystem that gates transaction confirmation on measured-versus-entitled weight tolerance, while avoiding the fragile centralized data stores of [10]-[12] by anchoring transaction hashes on the Ethereum Sepolia blockchain and layering AI-based anomaly detection absent from all three prior systems.

### D. Digital Governance in Welfare Systems

Beyond the technical architecture of digitized ration delivery, a substantial body of scholarship interrogates how digital identity and algorithmic governance actually function once deployed among India's poorest households, and this literature is essential for grounding any proposed supervision layer in the socio-technical realities of the PDS. Nagaraj and Prakash [13] investigate why Aadhaar-enabled biometric authentication, despite its alignment with global legal-identity development goals, has struggled to translate digitization into consistent welfare outcomes. Employing a complexity-theory lens and drawing on survey and interview data from roughly 1,600 below-poverty-line households across two states and four districts, their study finds that the centrally designed Aadhaar-enabled PDS (AePDS) is largely oblivious to the diversity of local social relationships, producing authentication failures, connectivity gaps, and biometric mismatches - particularly among elderly beneficiaries or those engaged in manual labor - that trigger self-adaptive coping behaviors among frontline actors, often to the detriment of poorer households [13]. The scale and theoretical grounding of this fieldwork lend it considerable credibility, yet the work stops short of prescribing an alternative architecture, leaving open how welfare-delivery systems might formally incorporate local variability rather than enforcing a single rigid verification checkpoint [13].

Hundal and Chaudhuri [14] complement this account with a more explicitly exclusion-focused lens, examining beneficiaries' lived experiences of Aadhaar-linked ePOS verification in Andhra Pradesh and Karnataka through qualitative interviews. Where Nagaraj and Prakash frame the problem through complexity theory, Hundal and Chaudhuri foreground the paradox that a nominally neutral e-governance technology can itself become a mechanism of exclusion, documenting how device and connectivity failures at the point of biometric authentication deny legitimate beneficiaries their entitlements [14]. The comparative two-state design strengthens the empirical claim beyond a single-site anecdote, though the brevity of the conference format constrains deeper causal analysis, and, like [13], the paper does not propose a concrete technical remedy beyond calling for more inclusive authentication design [14].

Chaudhuri's later ethnographic study [15] shifts the analytical register further, arguing that AePDS algorithms should not be treated as fixed computational artifacts but as socio-technical practices "enacted" through networks of officials, machines, documents, and beneficiaries across three states. This performative framing usefully exposes the persistent gap between algorithmic design intent and operational reality, though its purely qualitative method offers no quantifiable measure of exclusion or error rates, limiting its use for benchmarking system-level interventions [15].

Collectively, these three studies converge on a common diagnosis: rigid, centralized biometric verification is brittle under real-world social and infrastructural conditions, and existing AePDS deployments lack auditable, tamper-evident records of the very human-algorithm interactions that determine welfare outcomes [13], [14], [15]. The proposed Digital Supervision Layer directly addresses this gap by retaining the existing e-POS/Aadhaar-linked delivery pathway rather than replacing it, while layering OTP- and QR-based time-bound authentication, IoT-based physical weight verification, and blockchain-anchored hash trails atop it - thereby avoiding sole reliance on a single point-of-failure biometric check and providing the kind of granular, tamper-evident audit trail of every transaction and human intervention that [13]-[15] identify as absent from current digital welfare governance.

### E. Aadhaar Authentication

Biometric identity verification through Aadhaar has been the principal technological lever India has used to attack ghost beneficiaries and diversion within the PDS, and three complementary bodies of work reveal both the promise and the fragility of this approach. Muralidharan, Niehaus, and Sukhtankar [16] provide the most rigorous causal evidence, combining a large-scale randomized controlled trial with a natural experiment around the staggered rollout of Aadhaar-Based Biometric Authentication (ABBA) across Jharkhand, matching fingerprint or facial biometrics captured at ePOS terminals against records seeded in the Aadhaar Central Identities Data Repository (CIDR) before authorizing a transaction. Their administrative and field data confirm that mandatory biometric gating measurably reduced leakage and corruption at scale [16], substantiating the core premise that identity-bound authentication can curb fraudulent claims. Yet the same study documents a severe cost: an estimated 1.5-2 million beneficiaries lost access to entitlements, temporarily or permanently, during the transition, with the harm traced not to the biometric technology itself but to how the rollout was managed - seeding errors, absent grievance redress, and rigid enforcement without fallback [16]. This tension between fraud reduction and inclusion is echoed and contextualized by Menon [18], whose exploratory survey of Ranchi district found that only about 52% of ration-card households successfully completed a purchase in the study month, with failures attributable to incomplete Aadhaar seeding, biometric recognition failures, and the outright absence of offline or exception-handling mechanisms [18]. Where [16] establishes the causal magnitude of exclusion at a state-wide policy level, [18] localizes the mechanism, showing administrative and technical failure modes at the transaction level that a purely technical CIDR-matching architecture cannot resolve on its own. Nagaraj and Prakash [17] extend this critique theoretically, arguing through complexity theory and qualitative fieldwork across Jharkhand and Karnataka that the Aadhaar-enabled PDS (AePDS) architecture is a one-size-fits-all design that neglects the "local" - the emergent, heterogeneous social relationships between beneficiaries and fair-price-shop dealers - even though, when functioning correctly, ABBA does curb dealer discretion and duplicate-beneficiary fraud [17]. Notably, none of the three works proposes or evaluates a concrete remedy: [16] identifies implementation protocol as the lever without specifying what a better protocol looks like, [17] calls for context-sensitive design without instantiating one, and [18] documents failure modes without offering a fallback architecture.

The proposed Digital Supervision Layer directly inherits the diagnosis common to [16]-[18]: biometric-style gating is valuable for fraud suppression but dangerous if it becomes the sole, unforgiving arbiter of access. Accordingly, the system deliberately avoids introducing a new hard biometric bottleneck at the point of dispensation; instead, it layers OTP-based login, QR-code time-bound tokens, and JWT-secured sessions atop the existing Aadhaar-linked e-POS infrastructure, while shifting the anti-fraud burden onto IoT-based weight verification and blockchain-anchored audit trails. This design choice preserves the leakage-reduction benefits documented in [16] and [18] without replicating the seeding-error and no-fallback failure modes they expose, and it addresses the "local" heterogeneity concern of [17] by keeping human-supervisable, AI-flagged anomaly review in the loop rather than an automated, irrevocable authentication gate.

### F. QR-based Authentication Systems

QR-code-based credentials have emerged as a lightweight alternative to physical tokens, smart cards, and hardware OTP generators for verifying identity at the point of a transaction, a property directly relevant to authenticating shopkeepers and beneficiaries at ration distribution points. Kao et al. address this problem in the context of physical access control, arguing that keys, smart cards, and OTP fobs are costly to provision and easy to lose without immediate notice [19]. Their design fuses the one-time-usage guarantee of OTP with the information-carrying capacity of the QR code: a server issues a single-use secret encoded as a QR image to a registered mobile device, an access-control terminal scans the presented code, and the decoded value is validated server-side before entry is granted [19]. The scheme is attractive because it removes dependence on dedicated hardware, mitigates replay of an intercepted code through its one-time property, and repurposes a device most users already carry, thereby lowering deployment cost at each access point [19]. However, the treatment remains a system-design and security-discussion exercise rather than an empirically stress-tested deployment, and it presumes reliable mobile connectivity to a validation server at the moment of access — an assumption that is fragile in low-connectivity rural settings typical of fair-price shops, and one that leaves shoulder-surfing or code-photographing threats and nationwide-scale credential issuance largely unexamined [19].

Gangurde et al. tackle a related but distinct problem: rather than one-time access authorization, they secure the content of a reusable QR-coded credential — a train ticket — against forgery and tampering by encrypting passenger and journey details with AES before encoding them, so that only an authorized scanner holding the shared key can decrypt and validate the payload [20]. Architecturally, their three-tier structure separates a booking GUI, an AES-encryption/QR-generation backend, and a scanning/decryption verification module, illustrating how confidentiality can be layered onto QR issuance in a way Kao et al.'s purely OTP-based scheme does not attempt [20], [19]. This encryption-first approach meaningfully raises the bar against data tampering and reuses ubiquitous QR-scanning infrastructure, but the paper does not detail secure AES key distribution or rotation across many verification points, nor does it prevent a single encrypted ticket from being duplicated and redeemed more than once before first use, since no ledger records prior validation [20]. Where Kao et al. emphasize one-time consumability but weak content protection, Gangurde et al. emphasize content confidentiality but leave consumability and multi-point duplicate-redemption control largely unaddressed — neither integrates the credential with a tamper-evident distributed ledger or physical-quantity verification hardware [19], [20].

The proposed Digital Supervision Layer borrows the mobile-device-as-credential and time-bound, one-time-validity principles from Kao et al. for its QR-based authentication tokens [19], while structurally avoiding Gangurde et al.'s unresolved key-management and duplicate-redemption exposure by binding each token's validation outcome to blockchain-anchored transaction hashes and to the ESP32/HX711 weight-verification step, ensuring a beneficiary's entitlement QR cannot be silently reused or accepted without a corresponding, tamper-evident dispensing record [20], [19].

### G. Cryptographic Hash Chains

The conceptual foundation for tamper-evident audit trails, including the hash-linked anchoring approach adopted by the proposed hybrid blockchain trust layer, traces back to Haber and Stornetta's seminal work on digital time-stamping [21]. Their objective was to certify the creation or modification time of a digital document such that neither the document's owner nor a colluding time-stamping service could plausibly back-date or forward-date it, without requiring the service to retain document contents. To this end, they proposed two complementary constructions: a linking protocol that cryptographically chains the hash of each new submission to the hash-certificate of its immediate predecessor, yielding a totally-ordered, append-only sequence in which any retroactive alteration breaks all subsequent links, and a distributed witnessing protocol in which multiple independent parties attest to a hash. Relying solely on one-way collision-resistant hash functions and digital signatures, the scheme is remarkably lightweight and privacy-preserving, since it never stores the underlying content, but it depends on a single or small set of semi-trusted custodians and offers no Byzantine fault-tolerant consensus, no notion of value transfer, and no mechanism for pruning an ever-growing chain [21] - limitations that Nakamoto-style consensus and, more recently, application-specific tamper-evident logging systems have sought to address.

Building on this hash-chain primitive, Shekhtman and Waisbard's EngraveChain operationalizes tamper-evident logging using a permissioned distributed ledger rather than a centralized custodian [22]. By committing log-entry hashes to Hyperledger Fabric while layering encryption to shield log content from other participants, EngraveChain removes the single-point-of-failure risk inherent in [21]'s centralized time-stamping service and demonstrates feasibility across financial, medical, and security-access use cases using synthetic log data. However, its evaluation is confined to Hyperledger Fabric, leaving hybrid on-chain/off-chain scaling and high-throughput IoT log ingestion unexplored, and it incorporates no AI-driven anomaly detection or welfare-distribution application context [22]. Koisser and Sadeghi's Accountability of Things pushes further toward IoT-scale deployment by generalizing the hash chain into a timestamp-indexed binary hash tree, enabling constant per-device storage overhead (~8 KB/hour) and sub-second tamper localization across many interconnected smart devices [23]. Whereas [21] and [22] emphasize sequential or ledger-wide integrity, [23] targets resource-constrained device fleets, though it reintroduces centralized trust through its reliance on a trusted third party for root anchoring, without addressing hybrid blockchain integration or domain-specific fraud detection.

Collectively, these works trace an evolution from a single centralized hash chain [21], to distributed ledger-anchored logging [22], to scalable per-device hash-tree accountability [23], yet none combines hash-linked anchoring with a hybrid public/off-chain architecture, real-time IoT weight verification, or AI-based anomaly detection tailored to welfare distribution. The proposed Digital Supervision Layer borrows the core hash-linking principle from [21] and the on-chain hash-commitment strategy from [22], while avoiding both the single-custodian trust bottleneck of [21] and the permissioned-chain confinement of [22] by anchoring hashes on the public Ethereum Sepolia network. It further extends the IoT-scale accountability goals of [23] by pairing hash-anchored transaction records with ESP32/HX711-based physical weight verification and AI-driven anomaly detection, closing the integration gap these three works leave open for India's PDS.

### H. Ethereum Smart Contracts

Ethereum's programmability has made it the de facto substrate for research exploring how smart contracts can substitute for centralized recordkeeping in domains ranging from healthcare identity management to supply-chain logistics, though the surveyed literature diverges sharply on whether the emphasis is architectural design, application framing, or empirical cost analysis. Sahi et al. tackle the identity-management problem by constructing a semi-permissioned architecture in which a public Ethereum layer issues and manages self-sovereign identities while a Hyperledger Fabric permissioned layer handles access-controlled data exchange between hospitals and patients, bridged through a Keccak-based certificate-of-authority protocol and reinforced with multi-signature transactions and local-storage privacy guidelines [24]. The strength of this design lies in reconciling the openness and verifiability of a public chain with the confidentiality and throughput advantages of a permissioned one, eliminating dependence on siloed institutional databases and returning control of identity data to the individual [24]. However, the work is validated only through architectural design and a healthcare case study rather than production-scale deployment, and it offers no gas-cost, latency, or adversarial-security benchmarking, leaving the generalizability of its bridging mechanism to other public-sector contexts, such as beneficiary identity verification in welfare systems, unexamined [24].

Yigit and Dag shift the application domain to supply-chain management, proposing a Solidity-based smart-contract framework intended to encode data models and functions for recording and verifying supply-chain events on Ethereum, thereby improving traceability, transparency, and accountability while removing intermediary dependence [25]. Their contribution is conceptual rather than empirical: no dataset or live deployment is evaluated, and the authors themselves concede that gas fees and Ethereum Virtual Machine scalability remain open concerns, deferring exploration of lower-cost platforms, gas-optimization techniques, and AI-driven demand forecasting to future work [25]. This gap is precisely where Ta and Do intervene, offering a quantitative counterpoint by empirically measuring the gas cost of algorithmic smart contracts (e.g., quicksort versus bubble-sort logic) and simulating how block size and propagation delay affect throughput across Ethereum, Bitcoin, Litecoin, and Dogecoin [26]. Their findings substantiate the cost concerns that Yigit and Dag raise conceptually, yet because the workloads are generic algorithmic benchmarks run on simulators rather than live networks, the results stop short of characterizing gas and throughput behavior for domain-specific, production-grade public-sector contracts [26].

Read together, these three works trace a progression from architectural ambition [24], to application-layer conceptualization [25], to cost-performance quantification [26], but none directly addresses a welfare-disbursement setting with public-sector data-sensitivity and low-cost operational constraints. The proposed Digital Supervision Layer borrows the hybrid-architecture principle of [24] and the on-chain traceability rationale of [25], but restricts Ethereum (via Sepolia) to anchoring transaction hashes, timestamps, and identifiers rather than full identity or transactional payloads, keeping beneficiary data off-chain in PostgreSQL. This design is informed by the gas-cost sensitivities empirically demonstrated in [26], mitigating the very scalability and cost concerns that [25] and [26] identify as unresolved, while extending smart-contract-based auditability into the previously unaddressed domain of public distribution systems.

### I. Hybrid Blockchain Architecture

The application of hybrid on-chain/off-chain blockchain design to large, multi-stakeholder public systems has matured considerably from early conceptual proposals toward performance-validated architectures, and the trajectory of this maturation is instructive for a PDS-oriented trust layer. Singh et al. [27] were among the first to formalize this thinking specifically for the Indian PDS, proposing a consortium blockchain -- conceptually mapped onto Hyperledger Fabric -- in which smart contracts record and verify procurement receipts, warehouse transfer confirmations, and Fair Price Shop distribution logs on-chain, while bulk beneficiary records and analytics-ready supply-chain data remain off-chain, linked via cryptographic pointers. Arrived at through a design-science analysis of the existing procurement-storage-transportation-retail workflow rather than through prototyping, this model's principal contribution is architectural: it identifies precisely where PDS information exchange is currently unverifiable and proposes traceability, immutability, and stakeholder interoperability as remedies [27]. Its central weakness, however, is that no working system, testbed, or throughput/latency/cost evaluation accompanies the proposal, leaving scalability to India's actual beneficiary base and integration with legacy ration-card databases entirely unvalidated [27].

Miyachi and Mackey's hOCBS framework [29] generalizes this same on-chain/off-chain logic beyond PDS into healthcare, offering a more rigorous theoretical scaffold -- the Fit-for-Purpose design framework and three reference architectures for PHI, CHI, and genomic data -- that formalizes when data should be hashed on-chain versus stored in IPFS, Swarm, StorJ, or conventional databases, and how off-chain computation (SMPC, TEEs, verifiable computation) can mediate trust without bloating the ledger [29]. Like [27], this work remains purely conceptual, explicitly deferring empirical HOT-fit evaluation and prototype testing to future work, so neither source demonstrates that a hybrid design performs acceptably under real transactional load [29].

Sharmin et al. [28] close precisely this empirical gap, albeit in healthcare rather than PDS: their system encrypts records with AES-256-GCM, stores them on IPFS, batches SHA-256 hashes into Merkle trees, and commits only the Merkle root per 1,000-record batch to a Solidity smart contract deployed on Ethereum Sepolia, yielding a measured 99.85% reduction in gas cost against naive per-record hashing and throughput/latency figures benchmarked against five prior frameworks [28]. This is the only one of the three sources offering quantitative validation on a real testnet, though it concedes that IPFS upload/retrieval dominates latency and that unstructured, continuous IoT sensor streams would require additional preprocessing beyond its current structured-record design [28].

Collectively, these works establish the conceptual justification [27], [29] and the empirical feasibility [28] of hybrid on-chain/off-chain architectures, but none integrates real-time IoT-based physical verification or is validated against a national-scale, multi-agency public distribution workload. The proposed Digital Supervision Layer borrows the core hybrid principle -- anchoring only hashes, timestamps, and identifiers on Ethereum Sepolia while keeping operational and beneficiary data in PostgreSQL [27]-[29] -- but extends it by closing the on-chain/off-chain-to-physical-world gap through ESP32-based load-cell verification, directly addressing the integration and IoT-data limitations both [27] and [28] leave open.

### J. AI-based Fraud Detection

Applying machine learning and network analytics to detect fraudulent behavior in welfare and financial-benefit systems has matured considerably, and three complementary strands of this literature bear directly on the anomaly-detection needs of a PDS supervision layer. Kumar et al. address the specific problem of false income-level claims that let ineligible applicants masquerade as Below Poverty Line (BPL) beneficiaries, a fraud vector structurally identical to the misclassification risks NFSA category rules must guard against in the proposed entitlement engine [30]. Their approach benchmarks six classical classifiers -- Logistic Regression, Decision Tree, Random Forest, SVM, MLP, and Naive Bayes -- on applicant income and demographic data, with Random Forest emerging as the clear winner at 99.3% accuracy and a 0.99 F1-score [30]. The result demonstrates that even conventional, computationally light classifiers can outperform manual, paper-based verification that is otherwise susceptible to manipulation by middlemen and corrupt officials [30]; however, the study is confined to an offline, one-off classification exercise and relies on self-reported administrative income data, meaning the very data whose integrity is in question also trains the model, a circularity that leaves questions of real-time deployment, explainability, and adversarial robustness unresolved [30].

Alexopoulos et al. shift the unit of analysis from isolated records to relational structure, modeling real Value Added Tax transactions as a weighted business graph and applying spectral clustering and Laplacian eigen-decomposition to extract network features that are then fed into an XGBoost classifier [31]. This graph-theoretic technique surfaces coordinated, multi-party schemes such as carousel fraud that transaction-by-transaction statistical checks would miss entirely [31], a capability with clear analogues in PDS supply-chain diversion, where collusion among shopkeepers, transporters, and warehouse staff produces patterns invisible at the single-transaction level. Yet the method presupposes a fairly complete historical transaction network, degrades against sparse or new entities, and was validated only in a batch, offline tax-authority setting rather than a continuously streaming ledger [31] -- a gap Mienye and Jere's review implicitly reinforces from the deep-learning side. Surveying CNN, RNN, LSTM, and GRU architectures across the credit-card fraud literature, they consolidate recurring challenges of class imbalance, concept drift, and scarce public labeled data, along with mitigations such as resampling and cost-sensitive learning [32]. Because their synthesis remains a review rather than an original empirical deployment, and its domain is card transactions rather than welfare disbursement, it stops short of addressing identity duplication, biometric spoofing, or supply-side diversion characteristic of PDS fraud [32].

Collectively, these works progress from single-record classification [30], to relational/graph-based detection [31], to a broader deep-learning taxonomy [32], each improving detection sophistication while remaining offline, single-domain, and disconnected from tamper-evident infrastructure. The proposed Digital Supervision Layer borrows the classification and pattern-recognition logic these studies validate but embeds it as a continuous, real-time anomaly-detection module operating directly over hash-anchored, IoT-verified transaction streams -- closing precisely the integration gap [30], the real-time/cross-system gap [31], and the domain-adaptation gap [32] each identifies.

### K. Machine Learning for Anomaly Detection

The detection of irregular transactions within a Digital Supervision Layer for PDS is fundamentally a time-series and event-stream anomaly-detection problem, and the deep learning survey by Darban et al. offers the broadest conceptual grounding for this task [33]. Synthesizing a fragmented literature into a structured taxonomy organized by learning objective (forecasting-based, reconstruction-based, representation-based), architecture (LSTM, CNN, Transformer, GNN, autoencoder, GAN), and detection setting (univariate/multivariate, supervised/semi-supervised/unsupervised), the work equips designers with a principled vocabulary for selecting among competing model families rather than adopting one ad hoc [33]. Its chief strength lies in exposing evaluation-metric and benchmarking inconsistencies across studies, but because it is a meta-level survey rather than an empirical contribution, it validates nothing on domain-specific data, and its illustrative benchmarks are drawn from manufacturing and healthcare rather than welfare-distribution or transactional-governance settings [33]. Complementing this, DeMedeiros et al. narrow the lens specifically to IoT and sensor-network deployments, cataloguing techniques (GNNs, autoencoders, LSTMs, transformer-based detectors, traditional ML) against application domains such as intrusion detection, sensor-fault detection, and general time-series monitoring, while surveying benchmarks including SWaT, WaDI, SMAP, MSL, DS2OS, and N-BaIoT [34]. Where Darban et al. remain architecture-agnostic and domain-neutral, DeMedeiros et al. foreground practical deployment constraints—edge computation limits, heterogeneous and loosely connected sensor topologies, and a scarcity of well-benchmarked public IoT datasets—concerns directly pertinent to a load-cell-and-HX711-based verification layer operating on resource-constrained ESP32 hardware [34]. Notably, both surveys converge on the same structural gap: existing IoT anomaly-detection research is skewed toward security/intrusion anomalies rather than the logistical or operational irregularities (e.g., short-weighing, stock diversion) that a PDS supervision system must actually flag [34], [33].

Alarfaj et al. shift the discussion from sensor streams to transactional fraud detection, benchmarking classical ML classifiers (Decision Tree, Random Forest, SVM, XGBoost) against CNN and Gradient Boosting architectures on an imbalanced credit-card dataset, reporting accuracy near 99.9% and AUC near 98% [35]. Its explicit engagement with class imbalance is structurally analogous to the rarity of genuine fraud events within PDS ledgers, making its resampling and ensemble techniques transferable in principle [35]. Yet its validation is confined to a generic financial dataset, independent reproductions suggest possible overfitting between training and test accuracy, and the study neither addresses streaming/real-time inference nor IoT-originated heterogeneous inputs [35]—limitations the proposed system must resolve rather than inherit.

Collectively, these works supply the Digital Supervision Layer with a taxonomic map [33], IoT-specific architectural caution [34], and imbalance-aware transactional techniques [35], but none evaluate welfare-distribution data, label-scarce fraud settings, or the fusion of blockchain-anchored ledgers with real-time weight-sensor confirmation. The proposed anomaly-detection module borrows the reconstruction/forecasting model taxonomy and edge-deployment awareness of [33], [34] while adapting the imbalance-handling rigor of [35] to a domain these surveys leave unaddressed—flagging irregular ration transactions using synthetic PDS and IoT sensor data under real-time, resource-constrained conditions.

### L. Digital Wallet Systems

The concept of a blockchain-anchored digital wallet as a substitute for cash-based or purely paper-based subsidy disbursement has been explored by several researchers seeking to curb leakage in India's welfare delivery mechanisms, and their work bears directly on the entitlement-wallet component of the proposed supervision layer. Jaffer et al. [36] motivate their proposal with the striking observation that of the roughly $60 billion the Government of India spent on welfare subsidies in 2018-19, an estimated 40% was lost to misuse and inefficiency, and they trace this to structural weaknesses in the Aadhaar-linked Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT) process introduced in 2013 -- namely, weak auditability, an inability to restrict the end-use of transferred funds, and dependence on rural banking infrastructure that is often underdeveloped. Their remedy is a permissioned blockchain shared between the issuing government agency and redeeming vendors, in which Aadhaar numbers are cryptographically hashed to mint purpose-restricted digital tokens that function as an entitlement wallet rather than unrestricted cash, with every issuance and redemption immutably logged for reconciliation [36]. Bakare et al. [37] pursue a structurally similar goal in the agricultural subsidy domain, replacing intermediated bank- and agent-based DBT disbursement with a peer-to-peer, smart-contract-governed transfer directly between government and farmer, arguing that this removes the very intermediary layer through which corruption and delay have historically entered the system [37]. Both works are compelling in their diagnosis and in their use of cryptographic hashing and smart contracts to enforce purpose-restriction and auditability, yet both remain conceptual architectures validated only through design description rather than field deployment, leaving unanswered how such wallets would perform at national PDS scale, integrate with existing PFMS rails, or serve beneficiaries without smartphones or reliable connectivity [36], [37].

Pawar et al. [38] advance the discourse methodologically by grounding their blockchain-based food-subsidy distribution design in Value-Focused Thinking, eliciting design requirements directly from government, distributor, retailer, and beneficiary stakeholders before implementing a working prototype -- a stakeholder-driven approach that lends the resulting system greater real-world relevance than the purely technology-first framings of [36] and [37]. Even so, the prototype in [38] stops short of national-scale evaluation and, like the other two studies, incorporates no physical verification of dispensed quantities and no automated anomaly detection.

Collectively, [36]-[38] establish that a blockchain-anchored digital entitlement wallet is a credible substitute for cash-based subsidy transfer, and the proposed Digital Supervision Layer borrows this core idea -- hashing identifiers and anchoring transaction records on-chain -- while departing from these works in three concrete ways: it retains operational data in PostgreSQL and anchors only hashes on Sepolia for efficiency and privacy, it couples the wallet to IoT load-cell verification so that entitlement redemption is checked against physically dispensed weight rather than assumed, and it adds AI-based anomaly detection to flag irregular transactions, thereby closing the empirical, physical-verification, and scale-evaluation gaps left open in [36]-[38].

### M. Supply Chain Transparency

Traceability of physical goods across distributed, mutually distrusting stakeholders has become a proving ground for blockchain-based architectures, and three representative efforts illustrate both the promise and the recurring engineering compromises of this line of work. Wang et al. tackle the problem of incompatible partner-side information systems by embedding hash-chain data structures directly into RFID tags, using the tags as an immutable, product-carried ledger and RFID readers as lightweight blockchain nodes atop a Hyperledger Fabric 2.0 platform, with per-stage channels for manufacturing, storage, delivery, and retail [39]. Validated through a beverage-industry case study, the design's chief virtue is decentralization without requiring uniform partner systems, low-cost software-level adoption, and simple product-ID lookup for consumers [39]; yet its reliance on Byzantine fault-tolerant consensus raises scalability doubts at real supply-chain throughput, RFID memory bounds how much history each tag can hold, and the scheme offers no bridge for stakeholders lacking RFID infrastructure, remaining an untested prototype [39]. Wang et al. address a complementary problem-architectural complexity and storage-performance degradation-by fusing domain-driven design with microservices and a master-slave multi-chain framework on Hyperledger Fabric, partitioning the system into six bounded contexts (authority, records, warehousing, inventory, supervision, traceability) and offloading bulk data to IPFS with only hashes retained on-chain, a structure evaluated via ATAM and validated on a salmon-supply prototype [40]. This modularity improves maintainability and enables parallel per-domain processing and team development [40], but it trades interoperability for storage efficiency, incurs greater overhead than conventional databases such as MongoDB, and was validated in only a single domain, leaving generalizability and cross-service consistency management as open concerns [40]. Omar et al., in contrast, target a fully public setting-COVID-19 PPE traceability-through a three-tier Ethereum smart-contract design (Registration, Order Management, Lot contracts) paired with IPFS/Filecoin off-chain storage, tested in Remix IDE with explicit gas-cost and security analysis [41]. Its sub-$1.70 transaction costs and generic, adaptable contract structure are notable strengths [41], yet Ethereum's roughly 15 transactions-per-second ceiling, validation latency, and the absence of mainnet deployment or IoT/RFID integration for physical-layer verification mirror the scalability and real-world-validation gaps seen in [39] and [40].

Across all three, a consistent pattern emerges: hash-anchored, off-chain-heavy designs achieve tamper-evidence and stakeholder transparency but stop short of physically verifying that recorded quantities match delivered goods, and none progress beyond prototype-scale evaluation. The proposed Digital Supervision Layer borrows the hash-anchoring philosophy common to [39]-[41]-keeping bulk operational data in PostgreSQL while anchoring hashes on Ethereum Sepolia-but closes the physical-verification gap explicitly flagged in [41] by coupling on-chain anchoring with ESP32/load-cell weight verification, while avoiding the throughput and infrastructure burdens of full RFID-tag or multi-chain deployments required by [39] and [40].

### N. Role-Based Access Control

The foundational conceptualization of role-based access control originates with Sandhu et al., whose family of nested reference models — RBAC0 through RBAC3 — established the vocabulary and formal structure that nearly all subsequent RBAC systems, including blockchain-integrated variants, still build upon [42]. By decoupling permissions from individual user identities and mediating them instead through roles, sessions, and, in the higher-order models, hierarchies and separation-of-duty constraints, this work demonstrated that access administration in large, hierarchical organizations could be simplified while simultaneously enforcing least-privilege principles [42]. Its contribution is deliberately abstract: the paper offers no implementation, no performance data, and no treatment of enforcement across distributed, low-trust, or decentralized settings, leaving open precisely the question of how RBAC should be technically realized when multiple mutually distrusting agencies must share an access-control substrate [42] — a gap that motivated much of the blockchain-based RBAC literature that followed.

Ri, Kim, and Jong respond to that gap directly by encoding role assignment, activation, and separation-of-duty logic as smart contracts on a Hyperledger Fabric network, thereby relocating enforcement from a trusted central administrator to a permissioned distributed ledger [43]. Deployed and security-tested on a Fabric test network within a commercial cloud environment, their scheme demonstrates that RBAC constraints can be made immutable and auditable while mitigating insider-fraud risks through on-chain SoD verification [43]. However, because validation is confined to a single cloud testbed with known, vetted nodes, the work provides no throughput or latency analysis under consensus load and implicitly assumes a permissioned trust model that may not translate to multi-agency public-sector deployments where roles are static and context-blind [43]. Dadhania and Patel extend the decentralized RBAC paradigm into a domain-specific setting, proposing e-DRBAC-HC for healthcare, which layers IoT-sourced patient data and token-based encryption/verification atop a blockchain ledger to replace centralized EHR gatekeeping [44]. Their empirical evaluation, measuring encryption-decryption time, token verification latency, and RBAC policy execution speed against a baseline, lends this framework a stronger performance-validation footing than [43], and its explicit incorporation of IoT device input as an access trigger begins to bridge the physical-verification gap that [42] and [43] leave unaddressed [44]. Yet its role taxonomy (patient, doctor, administrator) and architecture remain healthcare-specific, offering no analogue to the multi-tier state–district–block–dealer hierarchies, rural connectivity constraints, or Aadhaar-linked biometric verification central to PDS governance [44].

Collectively, these works trace a progression from purely conceptual RBAC theory [42] to blockchain-enforced permissioned implementations [43] to IoT-augmented, performance-validated decentralized frameworks [44], yet none targets government welfare distribution. The proposed Digital Supervision Layer borrows Sandhu et al.'s role-hierarchy and separation-of-duty logic [42] to structure its Admin, Shopkeeper, and Beneficiary panels, adopts the smart-contract-enforced, tamper-evident verification philosophy of [43] for anchoring role-gated transaction hashes on Sepolia, and follows [44] in coupling RBAC with IoT-sourced physical data — here, load-cell weight verification rather than patient telemetry — while adding OTP, QR-based time-bound tokens, and JWT session management to address the rural, multi-agency access conditions those three works leave unresolved.

### O. Cloud-based Government Systems

A substantial body of research addresses the migration of citizen-facing and back-office government services to cloud infrastructure, and this literature is directly relevant to a PDS supervision layer because any hybrid blockchain design still depends on a conventional, cloud-hosted relational store for high-frequency operational data. Smitha, Thomas, and Chitharanjan conducted an early but still widely cited survey of cloud-based e-governance systems, cataloguing the service models — Infrastructure-as-a-Service, Platform-as-a-Service, and Software-as-a-Service — through which state governments were beginning to consolidate previously fragmented departmental servers [45]. Their research objective was descriptive rather than experimental: to map adoption patterns rather than benchmark a specific architecture, and the methodology consisted of a structured review of contemporary government cloud deployments across multiple countries. The technology discussed is generic virtualised cloud infrastructure rather than any single vendor stack, and no formal dataset or quantitative evaluation is presented, which is the paper's principal limitation when judged against later, more empirical work. Its enduring contribution is the identification of a G-Cloud (Government Cloud) model as the appropriate abstraction for public-sector workloads — a model that anticipates the architecture eventually adopted by India's MeghRaj initiative and by NIC-hosted state data centres.

Liang extended this line of inquiry with a specific architectural proposal for a government cloud intended to improve service efficiency, framing the research objective around reducing departmental infrastructure duplication through shared computing resources [46]. The methodology combined a conceptual system architecture with a discussion of expected efficiency gains, though — as with much early government-cloud literature — the evaluation remains largely qualitative. The paper's advantage lies in explicitly connecting cloud consolidation to service-quality outcomes for citizens rather than treating cost reduction as an end in itself; its limitation is the absence of any security threat model, an omission that later research treats as a first-order design concern rather than an afterthought.

More recent work has shifted the emphasis from adoption and efficiency toward risk and governance. Mkhatshwa and Mawela examine public-sector *perceptions* of cloud computing risk, using a qualitative methodology grounded in interviews with government IT stakeholders to identify data sovereignty, vendor lock-in, and multi-tenancy exposure as the dominant concerns inhibiting full cloud migration in public administration [47]. This research is valuable precisely because it documents the institutional hesitation that a purely technical proposal can overlook: even a well-designed hybrid blockchain-cloud architecture for PDS must contend with departmental reluctance to relinquish direct control over citizen data, a socio-technical gap rather than a purely engineering one.

The most architecturally relevant recent contribution comes from Rodrigues, Mateus, and Leithardt, whose CloudGuardian proposal integrates an Ethereum blockchain layer with a PostgreSQL-hosted cloud database to achieve bidirectional data synchronisation between an immutable ledger and a conventional relational store [48]. Their stated objective is to combine blockchain's tamper-evidence with PostgreSQL's query efficiency and storage scalability, using a synchronisation layer that writes security-critical events to Ethereum while routine transactional data remains in the cloud-hosted database — an architecture directly analogous to the hybrid design proposed for the PDS Digital Supervision Layer in this paper. The reported advantage is that neither component becomes a bottleneck for the other: high-frequency operations are absorbed by PostgreSQL while only security-relevant checkpoints incur blockchain gas costs. The acknowledged limitation is that the synchronisation layer itself becomes a new point of trust — if compromised, it can desynchronise the two stores without either one independently detecting the discrepancy, a concern this survey returns to in Section VI.

Taken together, the cloud-government literature establishes that (i) shared, virtualised infrastructure is now the accepted baseline for public-sector computing rather than a novel proposition, (ii) institutional risk perception — not technical feasibility — is increasingly the binding constraint on adoption, and (iii) hybrid cloud–blockchain synchronisation, while promising, has not yet been evaluated specifically in a food-security or welfare-distribution context, leaving open the research gap this survey's proposed architecture is intended to address.

## III. BASE PAPER SELECTION

The base papers selected below were chosen to span the four architectural pillars of the proposed Digital Supervision Layer - blockchain-based tamper-evident audit trails, IoT-based physical verification, resilient beneficiary authentication, and AI-driven anomaly monitoring - rather than clustering around a single technology. Each paper represents either the closest prior attempt at part of this architecture or the clearest articulation of a gap that the proposed hybrid PERN-blockchain-IoT-AI system is designed to close.

**Paper [6] - Consortium Blockchain Conceptual Model for Indian PDS:** This paper is selected because it is the most directly relevant PDS-specific blockchain proposal, offering a consortium (permissioned) blockchain design with an explicit on-chain/off-chain split for trusted data management. Its architecture directly informs the proposed system's hybrid blockchain trust layer, in which sensitive beneficiary and operational data remain in PostgreSQL while only transaction hashes, timestamps, and identifiers are anchored on the Ethereum Sepolia network - a concrete realization of the on-chain/off-chain separation that Singh et al. describe only conceptually. Because the original work stops at the design stage without empirical implementation, the proposed system extends it by building and demonstrating a working smart-contract-based anchoring mechanism, integrating it with a live PostgreSQL backend, IoT sensor inputs, and role-based application interfaces that were never operationalized in the conceptual model.

**Paper [4] - Smart Ration Shop Using Blockchain and IoT:** This paper is selected for being the closest existing prototype that jointly combines blockchain and IoT for ration distribution, using a permissioned Ethereum network, smart contracts, and Raspberry-Pi-based QR-code asset tracking. Its integrated blockchain-plus-IoT design directly informs the proposed system's decision to pair an Ethereum-anchored trust layer with a physical sensing layer at the point of dispensation, validating the general feasibility of this hybrid approach. However, because Malathi et al.'s framework was demonstrated only at small village scale with a fixed-capacity Proof-of-Authority network and QR-based tracking rather than direct quantity verification, the proposed system extends it by introducing real-time load-cell-based weight verification through an ESP32/HX711 module and by targeting a scalable, multi-panel (Admin/Shopkeeper/Beneficiary) architecture intended for broader deployment.

**Paper [2] - Aadhaar Biometric Authentication and the Neglect of the 'Local':** This paper is selected because it articulates, through empirical fieldwork, the core weakness of relying solely on centralized fingerprint-based authentication in the Aadhaar-enabled PDS (AePDS) - namely its fragility in the face of connectivity failures, biometric mismatches, and local heterogeneity. Its findings directly motivate the proposed system's authentication design, which deliberately avoids a single point-of-failure biometric gate in favor of OTP-based login, QR-code-based time-bound tokens, and JWT-secured sessions layered with role-based access control. Where Nagaraj and Prakash leave open the design of more resilient, context-aware verification architectures, the proposed system concretely instantiates such resilience through multi-modal, fallback-capable authentication mechanisms integrated with the blockchain and IoT layers rather than a single centralized biometric checkpoint.

**Paper [12] - Smart Ration Card Distribution System:** This paper is selected because its IoT architecture - built around a microcontroller, Aadhaar-linked biometric authentication, and automated dispensing sensors feeding a cloud database - is the closest prior system to the proposed physical verification layer, in which an ESP32 microcontroller with a load cell and HX711 amplifier weighs dispensed grain in real time. Indirani et al.'s use of sensor-actuator feedback for dispensation directly informs the proposed system's approach of gating transaction confirmation on physical measurement rather than trusting recorded quantities alone. The proposed system improves on this precedent by replacing the non-tamper-proof cloud spreadsheet used for record-keeping with a blockchain-anchored, hash-linked audit trail, and by adding an AI-based anomaly detection module atop the IoT sensor stream - both explicitly identified as unaddressed gaps in the original design.

**Paper [43] - Blockchain-Based RBAC Model with Separation of Duties:** This paper is selected because it demonstrates how role-based access control can be technically enforced within a permissioned blockchain (Hyperledger Fabric) environment using smart contracts and cloud infrastructure, providing a template for governing multi-stakeholder access in a distributed-ledger system. Its separation-of-duties enforcement mechanism informs the design of the proposed system's Admin Panel, Shopkeeper Panel, and Beneficiary Application, each of which requires clearly delineated, cryptographically enforceable permissions. The proposed system extends this model by embedding RBAC within a real-time, IoT-integrated welfare-distribution context - incorporating context-based access triggers such as QR-token validation and OTP verification - rather than the generic cloud-service access scenario for which Ri et al.'s model was originally designed.

**Paper [34] - AI-Based Anomaly Detection in IoT and Sensor Networks:** This paper is selected because it surveys machine learning and deep learning techniques specifically for detecting anomalies in heterogeneous, resource-constrained IoT sensor deployments, which closely matches the sensing environment of ration-shop weighing scales and transaction logs envisioned in the proposed system. Its emphasis on graph-based and unsupervised techniques for sparse, real-world sensor networks directly informs the design rationale for the proposed system's AI/analytics-based anomaly detection module, which flags irregular distribution patterns for administrative review. Whereas DeMedeiros et al. leave the application of these techniques to a concrete operational domain as an open problem, the proposed system grounds this methodology in a specific, deployable use case - correlating IoT weight-sensor data, blockchain-anchored transaction hashes, and entitlement records to detect diversion and short-weighing in near real time.

## V. DISCUSSION

The fifteen technology areas surveyed in Section II do not represent independent research trajectories so much as converging pressures on a single unresolved problem: how to make a high-volume, low-connectivity, socioeconomically sensitive distribution system simultaneously auditable, performant, and inclusive. Read across rather than within topics, several cross-cutting trends emerge.

**Technology trends and blockchain evolution.** The blockchain-in-PDS literature [4]-[6] shows a clear progression from early proof-of-concept permissioned ledgers, whose main contribution was demonstrating feasibility on small pilot deployments, toward hybrid designs [6], [28], [29] that selectively anchor only high-value events on-chain while leaving bulk transactional data in conventional databases. This mirrors almost exactly the architectural direction taken in the CloudGuardian proposal reviewed in Subsection O [48], and it reflects a broader realisation across the blockchain-for-government literature that full on-chain storage of every transaction is neither economically nor technically viable at national PDS scale — the gas-cost and throughput ceilings documented in the Ethereum smart-contract literature [24]-[26] make this explicit.

**IoT evolution and physical-layer verification.** The IoT-based smart ration literature [10]-[12] addresses a failure mode that blockchain alone cannot solve: a cryptographically perfect ledger entry recording "5 kg dispensed" is still fraudulent if only 4 kg physically left the shop. Weight-sensor and load-cell integration therefore functions as the *ground-truth input* to whatever ledger — centralised, blockchain, or hybrid — ultimately records the transaction. The comparative weakness across this literature is that most pilots evaluate the sensor hardware in isolation, without integrating it into an end-to-end authenticated dispensation flow that also verifies *who* received the grain.

**Hybrid architectures as the emerging consensus.** Considered together, the hybrid-blockchain [6], [28], [29], cryptographic hash-chain [21]-[23], and cloud-government [45]-[48] literatures converge on the same structural answer: a conventional relational database (commonly PostgreSQL) remains the system of record for day-to-day operations, while a separate append-only or blockchain-anchored layer provides tamper-evidence for the subset of events that matter for audit — dispensation confirmations, stock adjustments, and authentication events. What differs between proposals is *where* the boundary is drawn between "recorded" and "anchored" data, and none of the surveyed papers evaluate that boundary specifically for a food-subsidy context with NFSA-scale transaction volumes.

**Digital governance and adoption.** The digital-governance literature [13]-[15] and the cloud-risk literature [47] together indicate that the binding constraint on deployment is increasingly institutional rather than technical: departments hesitate to adopt architectures that reduce their own discretionary control over records, even when that reduction is the explicit security objective. Any proposed system must therefore be evaluated not only on cryptographic soundness but on how gracefully it accommodates legitimate departmental oversight functions (grievance redressal, corrective stock adjustment) without reopening the tamper surface it was designed to close.

**Security mechanisms and authentication techniques.** Aadhaar-based biometric authentication [16]-[18] remains the default identity layer for PDS but continues to exhibit non-trivial false-rejection rates for elderly and manual-labour beneficiaries; QR-based authentication [19], [20] and OTP/JWT-based session mechanisms are increasingly proposed as complementary or fallback channels rather than replacements. Role-based access control [42]-[44] research is comparatively mature but rarely integrated with the blockchain and IoT layers discussed elsewhere — most RBAC papers treat access control as a conventional web-application concern, disconnected from ledger-write permissions.

**Anomaly detection, scalability, and performance.** The AI-based fraud detection [30]-[32] and ML anomaly detection [33]-[35] literatures report strong classification performance on historical transaction datasets, but almost universally in offline, retrospective settings rather than as a live monitoring layer operating on streaming dispensation events. Scalability discussions across the blockchain and hybrid-architecture papers [4]-[6], [28], [29] consistently identify transaction throughput and gas cost as the dominant constraints, reinforcing the case for restricting on-chain writes to audit checkpoints rather than full transaction logs.

**Deployment challenges and government adoption.** Recurring across nearly every subsection is the observation that pilot-scale technical success does not translate automatically into state-level deployment; connectivity gaps in rural FPS locations, departmental change-resistance [47], and the absence of standardised interoperability protocols between state civil-supply systems are cited more often as deployment blockers than any cryptographic or algorithmic shortcoming.

**Future research opportunities.** The discussion above points toward a specific, underexplored research direction: a hybrid architecture that (i) uses IoT weight validation as ground-truth input, (ii) authenticates beneficiaries through a QR/OTP/Aadhaar-backed multi-factor flow, (iii) anchors only dispensation-confirmation events to a public or consortium blockchain via hash-linking rather than full data replication, and (iv) layers real-time ML-based anomaly detection directly on the transaction stream rather than on retrospective batch data. No single paper reviewed in Section II combines all four elements, which is precisely the gap the proposed Digital Supervision Layer is designed to close.

## VI. RESEARCH GAPS

Based on the critical analysis in Sections II, IV, and V, the following twelve research gaps are identified as unresolved in the current literature on digital PDS and comparable welfare-distribution systems.

1. **No real-time quantity verification.** Existing digital PDS records confirm that a transaction *occurred* but rarely verify, at the moment of dispensation, that the *physical quantity* dispensed matches the recorded quantity. IoT weight-validation research [10]-[12] addresses this in isolation but has not been integrated into an authenticated, ledger-anchored dispensation flow.

2. **No tamper-proof audit trail at national scale.** Blockchain-in-PDS pilots [4]-[6] demonstrate tamper-evidence at small transaction volumes but do not evaluate throughput or cost at the scale of a state or national NFSA rollout, leaving the question of production-scale tamper-proofing effectively open.

3. **Weak interoperability between state systems.** ONORC-style portability requires cross-state data exchange, yet the digital-governance literature [13]-[15] documents persistent format and schema inconsistencies between state civil-supply databases that no surveyed technical proposal directly resolves.

4. **Poor scalability of full on-chain storage.** Ethereum smart-contract literature [24]-[26] consistently reports gas-cost and block-time constraints that make full transaction-level on-chain storage impractical, yet many blockchain-PDS proposals do not specify which data subset should be selectively anchored.

5. **High blockchain transaction cost.** Related to the above, cost-per-transaction on public chains such as Ethereum mainnet remains prohibitive for millions of daily low-value ration transactions, and the surveyed literature offers no PDS-specific cost model comparing consortium-chain, sidechain, and hash-anchoring alternatives.

6. **Authentication failures under biometric strain.** Aadhaar-based authentication [16]-[18] continues to exhibit false-rejection issues among elderly and manual-labour populations; no surveyed paper proposes a validated fallback hierarchy (e.g., QR/OTP escalation) specifically calibrated for PDS demographics.

7. **Privacy concerns in ledger design.** Placing cardholder identifiers or family composition data on a public or consortium blockchain, even in hashed form, raises re-identification risks that the cryptographic hash-chain literature [21]-[23] acknowledges but does not resolve through a concrete data-minimisation schema for welfare systems.

8. **Limited real-time anomaly detection.** AI/ML fraud-detection research [30]-[35] is trained and evaluated almost exclusively on static historical datasets; none of the surveyed papers deploy a streaming anomaly-detection model against a live PDS transaction feed.

9. **Centralised points of failure in hybrid designs.** Hybrid blockchain-cloud proposals such as CloudGuardian [48] introduce a synchronisation layer between the ledger and the relational store; this layer is itself a new single point of failure that, if compromised, can desynchronise the two records undetected — a risk acknowledged but not mitigated in the surveyed architecture.

10. **Lack of hybrid solutions tailored to welfare distribution.** Most hybrid blockchain-cloud research [6], [28], [29], [45]-[48] targets generic enterprise or e-governance workloads; none evaluates the specific transaction pattern of PDS — high frequency, low value, geographically dispersed, intermittently connected — as a distinct design constraint.

11. **Limited IoT integration with authentication and ledger layers.** IoT weight-validation systems [10]-[12] are typically evaluated as standalone hardware pilots, disconnected from the QR/Aadhaar authentication layer [16]-[18], [19], [20] and from any ledger-write mechanism, leaving the three components technically unintegrated in the literature.

12. **Weak monitoring dashboards for real-time oversight.** Digital-governance and cloud-government papers [13]-[15], [45]-[48] describe periodic batch-reporting dashboards rather than continuous, transaction-level visualisation; no surveyed system couples a live anomaly-detection feed directly to a supervisory dashboard usable by civil-supply department auditors.

Collectively, these gaps indicate that the unresolved problem is not any single technology but the *integration* of physical verification, layered authentication, selective ledger anchoring, and real-time analytics into one coherent, cost-bounded architecture — the specific objective of the Digital Supervision Layer proposed in Section VII.

## VII. PROPOSED RESEARCH DIRECTION

The literature reviewed in Sections II, IV, V, and VI converges on a single diagnosis: every individual mechanism required to make PDS trustworthy already exists in some form, but no surveyed system integrates them into one coherent, deployable architecture. The Digital Supervision Layer proposed in this research is deliberately conceived not as a new algorithm but as a targeted synthesis that closes the twelve gaps identified in Section VI by combining eight architectural elements that the surveyed literature treats as separate research threads.

**Hybrid blockchain design.** The core architectural decision is to anchor only transaction hashes, timestamps, and identifiers on Ethereum Sepolia via smart contracts, while all operational data — beneficiary records, entitlement balances, transaction detail — remains in PostgreSQL. This directly closes Gap 2 (no tamper-proof audit trail at national scale), Gap 4 (poor scalability of full on-chain storage), and Gap 5 (high blockchain transaction cost), because the volume of on-chain writes is bounded by the number of dispensation *confirmations* rather than by the full transactional payload of a national welfare system. This improves on [4]-[6], whose permissioned-blockchain pilots demonstrate tamper-evidence only at small transaction volumes and never report throughput or cost figures at NFSA scale; on [6], [28], [29], whose hybrid designs establish the dual-store pattern but do not evaluate it against a high-frequency, low-value transaction profile such as PDS; and on [48], whose CloudGuardian architecture is the closest structural precedent — an Ethereum-plus-PostgreSQL synchronisation pattern — but was designed and evaluated for generic cloud-security use cases rather than for a welfare-distribution context, and, more importantly, leaves the synchronisation layer itself as an unaddressed point of trust. The proposed system inherits CloudGuardian's dual-store logic but narrows the on-chain payload specifically to hash-anchored dispensation events, which both reduces gas exposure below what [48] reports and gives the sync layer a smaller, more auditable surface to fail on.

**PERN stack as the operational backbone.** Choosing PostgreSQL, Express, React, and Node.js as the day-to-day system of record is a deliberate rejection of the temptation, visible in several early blockchain-PDS proposals [4]-[6], to push all data on-chain in pursuit of maximal transparency. The PERN stack gives the system the query performance, relational integrity, and mature tooling that a state-scale welfare system requires for its non-adversarial, high-frequency operations — entitlement calculation, wallet balance lookups, dashboard rendering — while reserving blockchain interaction for the comparatively rare, high-stakes event of confirming that a dispensation actually occurred. This division of labour is what allows the architecture to remain compatible with existing state civil-supply infrastructure rather than requiring a wholesale replacement, addressing the deployment-friction concerns raised throughout the digital-governance and cloud-adoption literature [13]-[15], [47] without asking departments to give up operational control of their own data.

**Hash-linked, tamper-evident ledger.** Beneath the Ethereum anchor sits a hash-chained ledger structure in which each dispensation record incorporates the hash of the preceding record, so that any retroactive alteration of a past entry breaks the chain of subsequent hashes and is immediately detectable without needing to re-verify every transaction against the blockchain. This closes Gap 1 (no real-time quantity verification, when combined with the IoT layer below) and reinforces Gap 2 by giving the system a lightweight, computationally cheap tamper-evidence mechanism that operates independently of blockchain confirmation latency. This improves on the cryptographic hash-chain literature [21]-[23], which establishes the append-only, chained-hash pattern but typically stops at single-writer trust — the chain proves internal consistency but offers no external, independently verifiable anchor. By pairing the hash chain with periodic Ethereum anchoring, the proposed system gives auditors an external checkpoint against which the entire internal chain can be validated, something the hash-chain-only designs in [21]-[23] cannot offer on their own.

**QR-code and OTP multi-factor authentication.** Beneficiary identity is verified through a combination of a QR code carrying a time-bound token and an OTP delivered to the registered mobile number, layered alongside — not replacing — existing Aadhaar-based verification. This directly closes Gap 6 (authentication failures under biometric strain) by providing a usable fallback for beneficiaries whose biometric readings fail, and contributes to closing Gap 7 (privacy concerns in ledger design) because QR tokens and OTPs are session-scoped and expire, unlike biometric templates, which remain a permanent and re-identifiable credential. This improves on [16]-[18], whose Aadhaar-centred literature documents persistent false-rejection rates among elderly and manual-labour beneficiaries without proposing a validated fallback hierarchy, and on [19], [20], whose QR-based authentication proposals are vulnerable to screenshot or replay attacks when deployed without OTP binding — a weakness the combined QR-plus-OTP flow in this architecture is specifically designed to close.

**JWT-secured sessions and role-based access control.** Admin, Shopkeeper, and Beneficiary interfaces are segmented through RBAC enforced at the API layer, with session state maintained via signed JWT tokens rather than server-side session storage. Critically, RBAC permissions in this design extend beyond the conventional web-application scope to govern *which roles may trigger a blockchain write* — a Shopkeeper can confirm a dispensation event, but only the automated entitlement engine can authorise the underlying wallet debit, and no human role can directly edit a hash-chained record. This closes Gap 9 (centralised points of failure in hybrid designs) by ensuring that no single compromised credential — including an Admin credential — can unilaterally rewrite ledger history, since ledger writes are triggered by validated system events rather than discretionary user action. This improves on [42]-[44], whose RBAC literature is mature at the web-application layer but is, as Section V observes, almost never extended to govern ledger-write permissions specifically, leaving a gap between access control and audit integrity that this architecture closes by design.

**Digital wallet and entitlement engine.** Monthly entitlements are computed automatically by NFSA category (AAY/BPL/APL) and credited to per-beneficiary digital wallets, with every debit hash-anchored at the moment of confirmed dispensation. This closes Gap 3 (weak interoperability between state systems) to the extent that entitlement logic is centralised and rule-driven rather than manually reconciled per state, and it closes Gap 7 by keeping wallet balances — not personal identifiers — as the primary on-chain-referenced data. This improves on [36]-[38], whose digital wallet research treats wallet integrity as entirely dependent on backend trust, with no external audit mechanism; anchoring wallet-debit events to the hash-linked ledger gives the wallet balance an independently verifiable history that [36]-[38]'s designs lack.

**IoT load-cell weight verification.** An ESP32 microcontroller paired with a load cell and HX711 amplifier weighs dispensed grain in real time, and the transaction is confirmed only when measured weight falls within tolerance of the entitled quantity. This closes Gap 1 directly, and closes Gap 11 (limited IoT integration with authentication and ledger layers) by wiring the weight sensor into the same transaction flow as the QR/OTP authentication step and the ledger write, rather than treating it as standalone hardware. This improves on [10]-[12], whose smart-ration-distribution pilots validate load-cell accuracy in isolation but do not connect the sensor reading to an authenticated beneficiary session or to a ledger-write trigger — meaning a correct weight reading in prior work has no cryptographic link to who received the grain, a link the proposed architecture establishes explicitly.

**AI-based anomaly detection.** Rather than the offline, retrospective evaluation pattern dominant in the surveyed literature, the analytics module in this architecture operates as a streaming layer over the live transaction feed, flagging irregular distribution patterns — repeated tolerance-boundary weight readings, unusual dispensation timing, or wallet-debit anomalies — for admin review as they occur. This closes Gap 8 (limited real-time anomaly detection) and contributes to Gap 12 (weak monitoring dashboards) by feeding flagged anomalies directly into the Admin panel rather than a separate offline report. This improves on [30]-[32] and [33]-[35], both of which report strong classification accuracy but exclusively on static historical datasets, with no surveyed deployment operating against a live transaction stream — the precise limitation this module is built to eliminate.

Considered individually, none of these eight elements is without precedent in the literature; considered together, no surveyed system combines more than two or three of them at once. The blockchain-PDS papers [4]-[6] implement ledger anchoring without IoT verification or streaming analytics; the IoT pilots [10]-[12] verify weight without any cryptographic ledger connection; the authentication literature [16]-[18], [19], [20] addresses identity without touching wallet integrity or RBAC-to-ledger mapping; and even the most architecturally advanced precedent, CloudGuardian [48], pairs blockchain and cloud storage without any physical-layer verification, multi-factor authentication, or anomaly detection at all. The Digital Supervision Layer's contribution is therefore integrative rather than inventive: it is the first design in the surveyed literature to couple physical verification (IoT), layered authentication (QR/OTP/Aadhaar), permission-gated ledger writes (RBAC/JWT), tamper-evident hybrid storage (hash chain + Ethereum anchoring), and real-time analytics (streaming anomaly detection) into a single transaction pipeline — closing all twelve identified research gaps simultaneously rather than the one or two that any prior individual system addresses.

## VIII. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK

This survey has reviewed fifteen technology areas and forty-eight sources spanning blockchain-based ledgers, IoT-based physical verification, biometric and token-based authentication, role-based access control, digital wallet systems, and AI-driven anomaly detection, all considered in the specific context of India's Public Distribution System and comparable welfare-distribution architectures. The central finding to emerge from this body of work is not a technology gap but an integration gap: the cryptographic, hardware, and analytical mechanisms required to make PDS auditable, resilient to fraud, and inclusive of beneficiaries with authentication difficulties already exist, individually validated, across a wide and maturing literature.

The recurring pattern across every subsection surveyed is that each proposed system solves its chosen problem in isolation. Blockchain-anchoring designs [4]-[6], [28], [29] establish tamper-evidence but leave physical dispensation unverified; IoT weight-sensor pilots [10]-[12] confirm quantity but are disconnected from any ledger or identity layer; authentication research [16]-[18], [19], [20] improves identity assurance without addressing wallet integrity or ledger-write permissions; and even CloudGuardian [48], the closest architectural precedent for a hybrid blockchain-cloud design, pairs Ethereum anchoring with PostgreSQL storage without any physical-layer verification, multi-factor authentication, or analytics layer at all. No surveyed system combines more than two or three of these mechanisms into a single deployable pipeline.

Read against this pattern, the twelve research gaps identified in Section VI collapse into four underlying deficiencies. First, verification and audit remain incomplete at scale: systems lack real-time quantity checks, tamper-proof audit trails robust to national transaction volumes, and designs that avoid a single centralised point of failure. Second, cost and scalability constraints are unresolved: full on-chain storage does not scale economically, transaction costs remain high, and interoperability across state civil-supply systems is weak. Third, identity and access mechanisms are fragmented: biometric authentication fails under real-world conditions, ledger designs do not adequately protect beneficiary privacy, and IoT verification remains disconnected from both authentication and ledger-write logic. Fourth, monitoring capability lags behind detection capability: anomaly detection is evaluated retrospectively rather than in real time, dashboards remain batch-oriented, and no hybrid architecture has been evaluated specifically for the transaction profile of welfare distribution.

The Digital Supervision Layer proposed in Section VII is, to the best of this survey's knowledge, the first architecture in the reviewed literature to close all four gap groups simultaneously, by uniting hybrid blockchain anchoring, IoT-based physical verification, layered QR/OTP/Aadhaar authentication, ledger-aware RBAC, an auditable digital wallet, and streaming anomaly detection within one transaction pipeline rather than treating them as separate research problems.

Several directions remain open for future research beyond the scope of the present design. These include: federated learning approaches that would allow anomaly-detection models to be trained across multiple states without centralising raw beneficiary transaction data; layer-2 rollups or state-channel constructions that could reduce Ethereum anchoring cost further than hash-based checkpointing alone permits; satellite and remote-sensing-assisted stock auditing at the warehouse level, extending verification upstream of the shop-level checks this architecture targets; explainable-AI techniques that would allow administrative users to understand and trust individual fraud flags rather than treating the anomaly-detection module as a black box; and extension of the same hybrid ledger, IoT, and RBAC pattern to other Direct Benefit Transfer schemes beyond food subsidy, where similar leakage and authentication challenges persist.
